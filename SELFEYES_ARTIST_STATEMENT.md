# selfeyes
### Notes on what the photograph saw

A selfie is a picture of a person. Look more closely and it is also a picture
of a room. The cornea is a wet, curved mirror, and at the resolution our
cameras now capture, the world reflected in someone's eye is legible.
Furniture, windows, the photographer, the screen of the phone, the street
outside. None of this was the subject of the photograph. All of it was
photographed anyway.

I started this project because I wanted to see whether it would work. It
worked too easily. With a high-resolution image and a slow zoom into the eye,
the reflection emerges — a tiny, upside-down photograph that nobody composed,
that nobody intended, that nobody knew was being taken. The eye does not
curate. It records what it is pointed at.

The pun is the title: selfies become *self eyes*. But the joke turns serious
quickly. Every selfie is already a self eye. Every portrait you have ever
posted, anywhere, at full resolution, contains a tiny photograph of the room
you were standing in. So does every photo of a child blowing out candles,
every dating-app headshot, every cat sleeping on a windowsill. The information
has been there the whole time, leaking out at a resolution we couldn't quite
see, until the resolution caught up.

Sunglasses are worse. They are a bigger, more spherical mirror with no skin
around them to absorb stray light. In a good portrait of someone wearing
aviators, you can read the photographer's face like a passport photo. You can
see street signs. You can see whoever else is in the room.

---

The work in this exhibition is a sequence of these revealed images, drawn from
photographs in the public domain and from openly licensed contributions. The
selection includes human subjects, pets, and animals — the last especially,
because a cat in a photograph never consented to being photographed but also
never minded, and the reflection in its eye is sometimes the most complete
witness of a domestic moment that exists. A photograph of a sleeping dog is
also a photograph of the person who was photographing the sleeping dog,
watching them sleep.

The project deliberately does not use facial recognition. It identifies the
*location* of an eye in an image; it does not identify *whose* eye. This is a
small distinction technically and an enormous one ethically. The work could
not be made if the goal were to expose particular people. The goal is the
opposite: to show that exposure has already been happening, invisibly, to
everyone, and that you do not have to be famous or interesting or careless for
your photographs to contain more than you intended.

It would be possible to make this work by scraping social media. It would be a
different work. The decision to source only from images whose photographers
have explicitly licensed reuse — the Library of Congress, the Smithsonian, the
New York Public Library, photographers on Flickr who attached Creative Commons
licenses to their portraits — is part of the work. The piece argues that we
leak information without consent. It cannot itself be a piece that aggregates
strangers' faces without consent. The ethics of the source material are not a
disclaimer; they are an aesthetic position.

---

The historical photographs are perhaps the strangest part. There are
daguerreotypes from the 1840s in archival collections where you can zoom into
the sitter's eye and see the curtains of the studio. There are Depression-era
portraits in which the photographer is visible in the reflection — small,
hunched, holding a Speed Graphic. The leakage is not a recent problem. It is a
property of eyes and lenses, and it has been quietly happening since
photography began. We are only now in possession of screens that show us what
was already in the file.

This means the work spans a century and a half without changing in any
material way. A Brady portrait from 1860 leaks the same kind of information a
phone selfie leaks today: who was in the room, what the room looked like,
where the windows were. The technology of seeing has accelerated. The
technology of being seen has not changed at all.

---

The slow zoom — the gesture this work is built around — is the entire argument
made spatial. You begin with a face you recognize as a face. You move closer,
and the face stops being the subject; the eye becomes the subject. You move
closer still, and the eye stops being the subject; the reflection in the eye
becomes the subject. The hierarchy of what the photograph is *of* inverts. You
arrive at a small, blurred image of a room you have never been in, and you
realize the photograph was always of that room, and the face was a kind of
frame for it.

I would like a viewer to leave this work with a small adjustment in how they
look at photographs of people. Not paranoia. Not a decision to delete
anything. Just a noticing — that the world looks back at us through every
surface that can hold a reflection, that the eye is the most candid camera
anyone has ever owned, and that for as long as we have been photographing each
other, we have been photographing far more than that.

There is something quiet about it. The reflections are small. They are not
dramatic. Most of them are mundane — a window, a hallway, a tripod, a hand.
The drama is in their existence: that they were there the whole time, and
nobody looked.
